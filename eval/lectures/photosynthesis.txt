Photosynthesis converts light energy into chemical energy stored in glucose. It takes place in chloroplasts, which are organelles found mainly in leaf cells. Chlorophyll is the green pigment that absorbs mostly red and blue light and reflects green light. The light-dependent reactions occur in the thylakoid membranes and produce ATP and NADPH. Water molecules are split during the light-dependent reactions, which releases oxygen as a byproduct. The Calvin cycle uses ATP and NADPH to fix carbon dioxide into sugars. The stroma is the fluid surrounding the thylakoids where the Calvin cycle takes place. Rubisco is the enzyme that attaches carbon dioxide to a five-carbon molecule during carbon fixation. Stomata are small pores on the leaf surface that let carbon dioxide enter and water vapor exit. On hot, dry days plants close their stomata to conserve water, which slows photosynthesis. C4 and CAM plants have adaptations that reduce water loss in hot climates. The rate of photosynthesis increases with light intensity until another factor becomes limiting.
